THE EXCHANGE RATE OF YOUTH — findings
Build #69 · 2026-11-15 · Topic: fitness
Data: OpenPowerlifting bulk CSV, revision 6b8787a8, 2026-11-14: 4,067,854 rows.
Sample: 877,655 raw full-power meets, 338,398 lifters, 357 federations,
2000-2026. Men 582,662 meets, women 294,993.

1. THE EXCHANGE RATE. At 90 kg bodyweight, one year of aging costs a man
   1.3 kg of bodyweight at age 40, 1.4 kg at 50, 2.4 kg at 60 and 2.8 kg at 65.
   For a woman at 65 kg: 1.1 kg at 40, 1.6 kg at 50, 2.7 kg at 60 and 2.7 kg
   at 65. Under 30 the rate is negative: at 20, getting older still adds
   about 2.2% of total a year for men and 2.3% for women. The price of a
   year roughly doubles between 40 and 60.

2. BENCH IS CHEAP, DEADLIFT IS DEAR. At 45, buying back a year costs 0.9 kg
   on the bench, 1.4 kg on the squat and 1.8 kg on the deadlift. Bodyweight
   is about twice as effective at buying bench as deadlift. At 60 the
   ordering holds: bench 2.1, deadlift 2.4, squat 2.7 kg per year.

3. STRENGTH PEAKS AT 25-27. At fixed bodyweight the model puts peak total at
   27 for men (583 kg at 90 kg) and 25 for women (323 kg at 65 kg). The slide
   is slow at first: a man loses 2.8% of his total across his late twenties,
   6.7% across his late thirties, 8.2% across his late forties and 12.9%
   across his late fifties. Women: 3.8%, 5.8%, 9.0%, 12.5%.

4. THE CLIFF IS 52 FOR MEN, 44 FOR WOMEN. Piecewise-linear fit on the model
   curve: men decline 0.46% a year before 52 and 2.06% after, a 4.5x
   steepening. Women decline 0.28% a year before 44 and 1.53% after, a 5.5x
   steepening.

5. THE CROSS-SECTION OVERSTATES AGING 4x. Lifters who keep competing past 45
   lose only 0.20% a year (men, n=2,206, median within-lifter slope), against
   0.82% a year in the cross-sectional curve. Women past 45 are still
   gaining, +1.08% a year (n=1,091). The 55-year-olds at a meet are mostly
   people who started lifting at 50, so the age curve confounds aging with
   short training history. This is the year-on-year side of build #60's
   result: at equal training age, older lifters still total less (80% at
   55-59 vs 30-34), so age does cost strength in level terms. What it does
   not do is make a continuing lifter fall off a cliff year to year.

6. MASS NEVER STOPS PAYING. The marginal return to another kilo is about
   0.57% of total per kilo at 90 kg for men, and it stays there at 25, 40
   and 55. It never falls below 0.3% anywhere from 50 to 160 kg. Raw medians
   agree: men aged 40-49 total 485 kg at 70-80 kg bodyweight and 655.5 kg
   at 125 kg plus. There is no weight where gaining mass stops buying
   strength. The constraint on getting heavier is health, not the total.

7. TWO NUMBERS CARRY HALF THE SIGNAL. Age and bodyweight alone explain 47.5%
   of the variance in men's totals (R2 0.475; gradient boosting with the same
   two features reaches 0.51) and 28.4% of women's. Everything else, training,
   genetics, programming, lives in the other half.

CAVEATS. Cross-sectional model, not a causal claim: lifters choose their
bodyweight, and untested federations are in the sample. Ages 70+ are thin
(4,701 men, 1,645 women) and the spline wobbles there, so headline numbers
stop at 65. Within-lifter slopes are medians over self-selected long-career
lifters, not a random sample of everyone who ever lifted. Lifter identity is
name plus sex, with a guard that drops identities whose age runs backwards
by more than a year (5,477 identities removed).
