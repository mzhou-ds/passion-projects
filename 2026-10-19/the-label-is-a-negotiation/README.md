# The Label Is a Negotiation — the serving size decides what the label is allowed to say

**Musing with Mike · daily build #43 · 2026-10-19 · topic: nutrition**

A Nutrition Facts panel looks like a measurement. It is closer to a negotiation. Three numbers go in: what is actually in the food, how big a serving the maker chose to print, and the FDA rounding grid the result gets snapped to before you are allowed to see it. This build reads the manufacturers' own data for 629,114 current US branded products (USDA FoodData Central, Global Branded Food Products Database, April 2025 release, 249 categories, 28,608 brand owners) and asks a question nobody seems to have run at census scale: if the serving is a choice, how much of what the label says is the food, and how much is the serving?

The short answer: the famous claim lines are mostly honest, and the zero is not. Products do not pile up under the "low sodium" or "low calorie" lines any more than they pile up under lines that do not exist. But the printed 0 is a different economy. Tens of thousands of products print 0 for a nutrient their own submitted data says is in there, and roughly 99,000 zeros and "low" claims on the shelf exist only because the serving printed above them is smaller than the standard serving for that food's own category.

**Try it:** open [`explorer.html`](explorer.html). Pick a nutrient, set the real content per 100 g and the serving size, and watch the label change. The default is a breath mint that is about 95% sugar served at 0.49 g. The label prints 0 g sugar. Play with the serving slider and you can watch the same food become honest.

## Findings

**1. The famous thresholds barely bend anything, and that null result is the setup.** The natural first theory is that products bunch just under the FDA claim lines: low sodium at 140 mg, low fat at 3 g, low calorie at 40 kcal, low cholesterol at 20 mg. So I measured the share of products near each line that fall just under it, and compared it to placebo lines where no claim exists. Sodium at 140 mg: 52.0% fall just under. Sodium at a meaningless 98 mg: 51.5%. Calories at 40: 50.5%, against 49.3% at a placebo 28. Fat at 3 g: 52.1%, against 54.0% at a placebo 2.1 g. Potassium at 140 mg, a line with no claim attached at all: 47.4%. The one exception is saturated fat at its 1 g line (61.8% just under, against 56.4% at the nearest placebo), a real but modest pile-up. The reason the big lines stay clean is structural: for most packaged foods the serving size is pinned by an FDA reference amount, so there is little room to lean on the line. The negotiation moved somewhere with more slack. See `charts/02_proximity.png`.

**2. The zero window holds 45,948 product-nutrient pairs, and 6,783 of them are not trace amounts.** US rules (21 CFR 101.9(c)) let a label print 0 for anything small enough per serving: under 0.5 g of any fat or sugar, under 5 mg of sodium, under 2 mg of cholesterol, under 5 kcal. Comparing each product's submitted per-100 g values against its own serving size, 45,948 pairs print that 0 while the maker's data shows the nutrient present. Saturated fat is the biggest pool (16,830 products), then sodium (10,584), total fat (8,038), calories (5,062), trans fat (3,585), cholesterol (1,168) and sugar (681). Most of those are genuinely trace, and the census counts them anyway, because the more damning subset is inside it: 6,783 pairs where the density is material. Sprinkles that submit 10 g of trans fat per 100 g print 0 g trans fat at a 4 g serving. A BBQ rub that is 45% sugar by weight prints 0 g sugar at a 1.1 g serving. Flavored oil sprays at 850 kcal per 100 g print 0 calories at a 0.2 g serving. This count is also a floor, not a ceiling: many makers submit the label values themselves back into the database (Tic Tac submits 0 g sugar per 100 g), so every zero-window case here is one where the manufacturer's own numbers contradict the printed zero. See `charts/01_zero_window.png` and `output/zero_window_examples.csv`.

**3. The serving is calibrated, sometimes to five decimal places.** Ferrero's Tic Tac line: 66 SKUs, serving size exactly 0.49 g, one hundredth of a gram under the 0.5 g sugar-free line, on a product that submits 388 kcal per 100 g. Conagra's olive, avocado and canola oil sprays submit 83.33 g of fat per 100 g and a 0.6 g serving, which puts fat per serving at 0.49998 g. Cooking sprays as a class are the purest case: 668 spray SKUs sit in the same category as 6,572 bottled oils, where the median product is 93 g of fat per 100 g served at 15 g for 120 kcal. The sprays' median serving is 0.25 g (the label literally says a third of a second), 86% of them submit 0 g of fat per 100 g for a product that is oil in a can, and 87% print 0 g fat and 0 calories at once. And 232 products are actually named "Zero Calorie Sweetener"; 58% of them submit sugar at 90 g or more per 100 g, median 100 g per 100 g at a 1 g serving. The packet is sugar. The zero is the serving. See `charts/05_spray.png`.

**4. Hideable capacity is a formula, and 2,048 products can hide a pure substance behind a zero.** What a printed 0 can legally conceal per 100 g is just the zero threshold divided by the serving size, times 100. At a 30 g serving, a 0 g fat label can hide at most 1.7 g per 100 g, which is a rounding courtesy. At a 1 g serving it can hide 50 g per 100 g. At a quarter-gram spray serving it can hide 200 g per 100 g, which is to say anything, including pure fat. Across the catalog, 43,107 products have servings small enough to hide 5 g or more per 100 g behind a fat or sugar zero, 16,011 can hide 25 g or more, 8,459 can hide 50 g or more, and 2,048 have sub-0.5 g servings where even a product that is 100% fat or 100% sugar prints 0. There are 24,067 products with servings under 5 g and 5,223 under 1 g. Nobody eats a third of a second of anything, but the label only ever meets the serving, never the meal. See `charts/03_hideable.png`.

**5. Relabel everything at the standard serving and about 99,000 labels change.** The counterfactual: keep every recipe identical, but print each product's nutrients at its own category's median serving, the standard its competitors mostly use. 16,680 printed zeros disappear (7,023 of them saturated fat, 3,501 total fat, 2,616 trans fat, 2,487 calories). Claim eligibility falls harder: 28,063 products lose low sodium, 18,429 lose low fat, 17,277 lose low saturated fat, and 18,414 lose low calorie, which is 15.7% of everything currently eligible to say it. None of those products changed. Only the serving did. That is the cleanest statement of the finding: on the order of 99,000 labels on the American shelf are not descriptions of food. They are descriptions of a serving size. See `charts/04_counterfactual.png`.

**6. Asked to predict a printed zero, a model listens to the serving almost as much as to the food.** Among products that contain any fat, an XGBoost model predicting whether the label prints 0 g fat gets AUC 0.962 from the fat content alone and 0.826 from the serving size alone; SHAP ranks content first and serving size a clear second, ahead of calorie density and every category. For calories the order nearly flips: whether a product with real calories prints "0 calories" is predicted better by serving size alone (AUC 0.926) than by the product's actual calorie density (0.909). (With both inputs the answer is arithmetic by construction, AUC 1.0; the ablations are the informative part.) The label is a function of two variables, and for the zero that shoppers trust most, the variable the maker controls contributes about as much signal as the variable that is the food. See `charts/06_shap.png`.

**7. Even the honest numbers are snapped to a coarse grid, and you can measure the grid.** Labels do not print measurements, they print grid points: fats in half grams, cholesterol in 5 mg steps, fiber in whole grams. In the reconstructed data, 98.3% of nonzero total fat values sit exactly on the half-gram grid, 97.3% of saturated fat, 92.6% of fiber on the whole-gram grid, against 60.5% for potassium on a comparable grid. A printed "1 g" of saturated fat is a bucket roughly from 0.75 g to 1.25 g. This is disclosed policy, not deception, but it means the panel's precision is theater: the third significant figure on any label is a legal fiction, and the first one is a range.

## What would actually fix it

The data points at the cheap fix, and it is not more thresholds. Per-100 g (or per-100 ml) declaration, printed next to the per-serving panel the way most of the world already does it, kills every mechanism in this build at once: the zero window shrinks to actual zeros, the hideable capacity formula stops depending on a chosen serving, and the counterfactual in finding 5 becomes the label itself. The US already computes these numbers. Every manufacturer in this database submitted them. They are simply not the numbers on the front of the panel.

## Methods and caveats

- Source: USDA FoodData Central, Global Branded Food Products Database, April 2025 bulk release. Filtered to market country United States, not discontinued, one row per GTIN (latest modified version), serving size reported in g or ml (629,114 of 734,528 nutrient-matched products; the rest mostly report servings in pieces or household units that cannot be converted exactly, and are excluded rather than guessed).
- Per-serving values are reconstructed as submitted per-100 g amount × serving size / 100. This reproduces the declared panel up to the submission rounding in the source data; the grid measurement in finding 7 (97%+ of values landing exactly on the legal grid) is the internal check that the reconstruction tracks the printed label.
- The zero-window census is a lower bound. Where a manufacturer's submission is itself derived from its label panel, a gamed zero enters the data as a true zero and cannot be detected from this source. Tic Tac (submits 0 g sugar per 100 g) and most cooking sprays (submit 0 g fat per 100 g) are in that blind spot, which is why they are documented from their serving sizes and category contrasts instead.
- "Material" tiers in finding 2 use per-100 g density cuts (trans fat ≥1, total fat ≥5, saturated fat ≥2, sugar ≥10, sodium ≥400 mg, cholesterol ≥10 mg, calories ≥50 kcal, all per 100 g). The cuts are judgment calls, stated here so they can be argued with; the full census without cuts is in `output/results.json`.
- The proximity scoreboard uses ±15–25% bands around each line with placebo lines and a no-claim nutrient as controls. It is a descriptive bunching check, not a fitted bunching estimator, and the saturated fat signal should be read as suggestive.
- The standard-serving counterfactual uses each branded food category's median serving across the whole catalog. A category median is a blunt standard (an FDA reference amount would be sharper but is not in this dataset); blunt cuts both ways and the direction of the bias is not obvious, so flip counts are reported as approximate.
- Duplicated listings (same product, multiple GTINs for sizes and multipacks) are counted as separate shelf products, because each one carries its own label in a store.

## Reproduce

```
python src/download.py   # USDA branded CSVs, April 2025 release (~440 MB)
python src/extract.py    # -> data/products.parquet (629,114 products)
python src/analyze.py    # -> output/results.json, examples, SHAP sample
python src/charts.py     # -> charts/*.png
```

Data files are gitignored (the raw release is ~3 GB). Code, charts, the explorer, and all headline numbers are in the repo.
