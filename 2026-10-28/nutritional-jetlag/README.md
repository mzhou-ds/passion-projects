# Nutritional Jetlag: You Differ More From Yesterday's You Than From a Stranger

**Musing with Mike · daily build #52 · 2026-10-28 · topic: nutrition**

Chrono-nutrition has a simple picture in mind. Each of us has an eating clock. Some people are early eaters, some are late eaters, and the research mostly asks whether the late ones pay for it in glucose, weight, and sleep. Nearly all of that research measures the clock once or twice and treats the reading as the person.

So I measured the clock twice for the same people and asked a prior question: how much does one person's eating clock move between two ordinary days? NHANES gives two non-consecutive 24-hour recalls for most participants, with a clock time and a name for every eating occasion. Across six cycles, 2007 to 2018, that is 24,139 adults aged 20 to 79 with two reliable recall days. For each day I built the clock three ways: first bite, last bite, and the calorie-weighted midpoint, the hour by which half the day's calories are in.

The answer is that the typical person is not running one clock with a little noise on it. The typical person is running a clock that lands in a different place almost every day, far enough that "early eater" and "late eater" are less traits than weather.

**Try it:** open [`explorer.html`](explorer.html), pick your age band and the kind of days you are comparing, and see how far the clock moves for people like you.

## Findings

**1. The median person's eating midpoint moves 1.26 hours between two ordinary days.** The calorie midpoint shifts by a median of 1.26 hours (interquartile range 0.58 to 2.22, mean 1.60). Almost a third of adults, 29.5%, move more than 2 hours. One in seven moves more than 3. The eating window itself changes by a median of 1.58 hours. First bite and last bite each move about an hour and a half on average. This is not a holiday or a red-eye in the data. These are the two most ordinary days a national survey could catch, a few days apart. See `charts/01_shift_distributions.png`.

**2. You differ more from yourself than people differ from each other.** This is the measurement underneath everything else. The within-person standard deviation of the midpoint is 1.55 hours. The between-person standard deviation, how much people's average midpoints actually spread out, is only 1.03 hours. Read that again. One person's day-to-day wobble is one and a half times wider than the entire spread of habits the field sorts people by. A person's average move between their two days (1.60 hours) is 77% as large as the average gap between two random strangers (2.07 hours). And 31% of people shift more between Tuesday and Friday than the distance between the 25th and 75th percentile eater in America. The signal chrono-nutrition studies chase is smaller than the noise inside each subject.

**3. One recall day is a bad photograph, and two days is not much better.** The correlation between a person's two days (the intraclass correlation) is 0.31 for the midpoint, 0.39 for first bite, 0.30 for last bite, 0.32 for window length, and just 0.26 for the share of calories eaten after 8pm. Total calories, the thing recalls were designed for, do better at 0.45. Even averaging both days only lifts the midpoint's reliability to 0.47. Run the Spearman-Brown arithmetic and you need about nine recall days to measure one person's eating midpoint at the reliability a decent survey scale would demand. Most meal-timing papers use one or two days. Their effect sizes are not wrong so much as shrunk, by a factor close to the ICC itself, before anyone argues about confounders. See `charts/02_reliability.png`.

**4. It is not the weekend.** The obvious suspect is the calendar: one recall lands on a Saturday, the other on a Tuesday, and of course the clock moves. The data lets that suspect off with a warning. People whose two days straddle the weekday/weekend line shift a median 1.31 hours. People whose days are both weekdays shift 1.21 hours. Six extra minutes is all the calendar buys. Inside the mixed pairs, the weekend day itself is barely a different animal: its midpoint runs 0.17 hours later, its last bite does not run later at all, and it carries 74 more calories. The wobble is not social jetlag wearing a food costume. It is there on plain Tuesdays. See `charts/03_pair_type.png`.

**5. The eating day does not slide. It stretches.** If the clock moved as one piece, a late first bite would predict a late last bite, and the window would hold its shape while the day drifted. The correlation between the signed first-bite shift and the signed last-bite shift is 0.06, which is nothing. Among people whose both ends moved at all, the two ends move in the same direction 50.6% of the time. A coin flip. Your eating day is less like a train running late and more like an accordion, opening and closing from both ends independently. Any intervention that assumes you can grab the whole day by its first bite and pull it earlier is pulling on one end of an accordion.

**6. The day your clock runs late is the day you eat more, and it is your own comparison.** Because each person is measured twice, the intake question can be asked within-person, where every stable habit, preference, and metabolism is held fixed by construction. Regressing the day-to-day calorie change on the day-to-day clock change: each hour the midpoint runs later comes with 38 more calories that day (SE 5). Each hour the window widens comes with 49 more (SE 3). Each 10-point rise in the share eaten after 8pm comes with 14 more. The binned version is easy to feel. When your midpoint lands one to two hours later than your own other day, you eat about 139 calories more than the average day-pair change. When it lands more than two hours earlier, about 107 fewer. This is not proof the clock causes the calories; a long day can stretch both. But it is the comparison the "just eat earlier" advice imagines, run inside one life instead of across strangers, and it survives. One honest wrinkle, handled in the method: second recalls average 102 calories lower than first recalls overall, a known reporting effect, so all of these numbers are estimated on changes with that level gap absorbed, not on levels. See `charts/05_delta_intake.png`.

**7. A third of the night eating wears a fake name.** After 8pm, 39% of all calories arrive at occasions the respondent called a "snack." On 16% of days, that snack is at least 300 calories, which is a meal by any honest count, and on 40% of days there is some post-8pm snack, a median 244 calories when it happens. The fourth meal is real, common, and hidden inside a word that makes it sound like a handful of chips. Diet recalls, food diaries, and most people's own memory of their day all file it under the small word.

**8. Almost nobody's demographics predict their wobble, and that is the point.** Age grades it gently. Twenty-somethings shift a median 1.45 hours, with 37% moving more than 2 hours, against 1.11 hours and 22% for seventy-somethings. After that the trail goes cold. An XGBoost model with demographics, BMI, income, education, marital status, the calendar mix, and each person's average clock explains 12% of the variance in shift size and barely beats guessing the median. Its SHAP ranking is topped not by who you are but by how you eat: the share of your calories after 8pm and how late your average midpoint sits. Late, loose evenings are the unstable part of the clock. And the instability is not new. Cycle medians sit between 1.21 and 1.34 hours from 2007 to 2018. Phones did not do this. People were always like this. The averages just hid it. See `charts/04_age.png` and `charts/06_shap.png`.

## The market read

A lot of money is being spent prescribing fixed eating windows to people whose windows move a median hour and a half a day. Time-restricted eating apps, chrono diets, and glucose-timing advice all take the average clock as the product surface: find your window, hold your window. This data says the variance is the product surface. The person who "eats in a 10-hour window" is really drawing a window from a distribution with a standard deviation over two hours, and the days the window stretches are the days the calories land. A coach, an app, or a GLP-1 companion program that measures and manages the stretch, the late loose evenings especially, is working on the number that actually moves intake. The research market has the mirror-image problem: a field built on one-day timing measures is pricing effects at a third of their size, and the first labs to fund nine-day timing protocols will find effects everyone else attenuated away.

## Method

- `src/download.py` fetches the NHANES 2007-08 through 2017-18 Individual Foods (DR1IFF, DR2IFF), Demographics, and Body Measures XPT files from CDC.
- `src/build_clocks.py` collapses food items into eating occasions (same respondent, same clock time) and builds each person-day's clock: first and last calorie-bearing occasion, calorie-weighted midpoint, window, total kcal, shares after 20:00 and 22:00, calories after 20:00 at snack-named occasions (codes 6, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18), dinner-named calories and time, and a breakfast flag. The pair file keeps adults 20 to 79 with both recalls rated reliable, each day between 500 and 6,000 kcal and at least two calorie occasions: 24,139 people.
- `src/analyze.py` computes weighted shift distributions (two-day dietary weights WTDR2D), one-way random-effects ICCs with within and between standard deviations, day-pair-type cuts (day-of-week from DRxDAY, 1 = Sunday), mixed-pair weekend-day contrasts, differenced (within-person) weighted regressions of intake change on clock change with robust standard errors, the stranger-gap simulation, and the evening snack accounting. Quantile standard errors come from a weighted bootstrap; first and last bite medians sit exactly on heaped values (see caveats), so their spread is reported by IQR.
- `src/model.py` fits XGBoost (500 trees, depth 3) predicting absolute midpoint shift from demographics, BMI, pair composition, cycle, and the person's average levels, with SHAP importances. Average levels are included deliberately: the question is whether anything observable, including your own usual clock, forecasts your instability.
- `src/charts.py` and `src/make_explorer.py` render the six charts and the explorer from the pair table.
- `data/person_pairs.parquet` is the derived pair table. Raw XPT files are git-ignored; the download script refetches them.

Reproduce: `pip install -r requirements.txt && python src/download.py && python src/build_clocks.py && python src/analyze.py && python src/model.py && python src/charts.py && python src/make_explorer.py`.

## Caveats, stated flatly

Recall times are heaped. Ninety-three percent of first bites land on an hour or half hour, because people round when they report, and some of the measured wobble is rounding noise moving around a stable truth. That inflates the shift a little, though rounding alone cannot manufacture the stretch result (the two ends would still correlate if the day slid) or the intake link. The two recalls are 3 to 10 days apart, so this measures short-run instability, not seasonal drift; long-run instability is presumably larger. Day 2 is collected by phone and averages about 102 fewer calories, a mode and fatigue effect the differenced regressions absorb with their intercept but the descriptive levels cannot fully escape. The midpoint treats an eating occasion at 1am as early rather than as the tail of the previous evening, which is the convention of the recall day; dropping everyone with a pre-4am first bite on either day (n = 22,825) leaves the median shift at 1.27 hours, so the headline does not rest on night eaters. Calories after 20:00 at "extended consumption" occasions are counted in the after-8 total but not as snacks, which makes the snack share a floor. And nothing here says a stable clock is healthier. It says the clock is not stable, which any claim about what a person's clock does has to survive first.
