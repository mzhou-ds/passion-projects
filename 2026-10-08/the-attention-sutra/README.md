# The Attention Sutra

**Mindfulness lost the decade. The winner is a Roman emperor who has been dead for more than 1,800 years — and the data says philosophy now sells the way software does: a person, a book, a January launch window, and a crisis.**

*Musing with Mike · 2026-10-08 · Topic: philosophy · Build #32*

I keep two shelves in my head. One is the Tao Te Ching, the Dhammapada, Emerson, Thoreau — the books that actually changed how I live. The other is whatever the internet is doing with philosophy right now, which mostly looks like Marcus Aurelius quotes over gym photos. I wanted to know if that second shelf is a distortion or the real market. So I measured the market. Ten years of monthly Wikipedia pageviews — January 2016 through September 2026, 129 months, 36 articles, 453 million views — sorted into five traditions: Stoicism, the Eastern shelf (Taoism, Buddhism, Zen, mindfulness, karma), transcendentalism, the existentialists, and a classical control group of Plato, Aristotle, Socrates, and Kant. Then I pulled the actual primary texts off Project Gutenberg and measured how they read. The question underneath all of it is an operator's question, the same one I asked at Lyft and DoorDash and Patreon: where is demand actually moving, and what is it buying?

## Key findings

**1. Stoicism is the only tradition that compounded.** In 2016 the Stoic shelf took 8.8% of this panel's attention. Lately it takes about 14–15% (15.3% once you remove the one article I had to quarantine — finding 5). It runs the strongest long-run trend in the panel, about +5% a year since 2017 (the Eastern shelf's +0.5% is entirely the Laozi anomaly of finding 5; quarantine him and it is flat), while transcendentalism bleeds roughly 3% a year and the classical shelf drifts down about 1% a year. The median Stoic article grew 59% between the late 2010s and 2023–26. Marcus Aurelius alone now pulls 234,000 views a month, up 83%. Seneca is up 70%. Epictetus, a man born enslaved, is up 52%. This is not a spike. It is a decade-long compounding curve, and compounding curves are what businesses are made of.

**2. COVID ran the experiment for us, and control beat calm.** When the world locked down, people did not reach for the wellness shelf. They reached for the control shelf. Stoicism attention jumped 32% in the COVID window against its pre-2020 baseline. Epictetus rose 41%, Viktor Frankl 34%, Man's Search for Meaning 41%. Mindfulness — the concept the apps were supposedly built on — *fell* 33% in the same window and is down 44% across the decade, the single biggest loser in the panel. Buddhism fell 29%, Zen 21%, karma 30%. My read: the demand for calm did not disappear, it changed channel. Nobody looks up "mindfulness" on an encyclopedia when Calm will just play them the session. Lookup intent collapsed because a product absorbed it. Meanwhile there is no app that does Stoicism for you, so the reading survived. Philosophy that can be productized into a subscription leaves the page. Philosophy that is a practice you have to do yourself stays in print.

**3. The -isms are dying. The people and the books are not.** Split the panel by what an article *is* and the pattern is blunt. Median decade growth: individual works +27%, individual people +6%, abstract concepts −10%. "Existentialism" the concept is down 38% while Frankl's book is up 63% and Camus is up 27%. "Transcendentalism" is down 32% while Emerson the man is basically flat. There is exactly one exception, and it proves the rule: "Stoicism" the concept is *up* 40%, because Stoicism is the one -ism that got turned into a brand — a daily practice, a calendar, a name you can put on a cover. Attention does not buy categories anymore. It buys a face and a text. Every marketplace I have ever worked on learned this same lesson the hard way: supply organizes around whoever makes the abstract thing concrete.

**4. Philosophy has seasons, and they are not the seasons you'd guess.** Stoicism's strongest month is January — 14% above its own non-January baseline, the biggest New Year effect of any tradition. People do not discover Marcus Aurelius, they *resolve* him. The classical shelf peaks in September, 12% above baseline, which is homework: Plato and Aristotle are a school economy, not a self-improvement one. Those are two different businesses wearing the same section of the bookstore. One sells to people trying to change. The other sells to people trying to pass. If you were launching anything in this space — a course, a book, a channel — the calendar in this data is the whole media plan: Stoic content in January, existential meaning when the world breaks, classical explainers in September.

**5. The most-viewed philosopher in the world is probably a bot, and catching that is half the job.** On paper, Laozi is the biggest winner in history: 299,000 views a month lately, up 591%, the #1 article in the whole panel. It is not real. His traffic sat near 45–55 thousand a month for seven years, then stepped 6.3× in a single month — March 2023, 49k to 361k — and sat at that altitude for two years before sagging. Human beings do not change their minds like a step function. That is automated or embedded referral traffic, and any analysis that reported it straight would have crowned a phantom. I quarantined Laozi from the headline numbers (the code flags step-changes ≥3× automatically; Confucius had a one-month 6× spike in September 2018 that washes out, Laozi's does not). The lesson travels well beyond philosophy: every attention dashboard has a Laozi in it, and the first job is finding yours before it makes your decisions.

**And the texts themselves? Style doesn't explain any of it.** I measured eight primary texts — sentence length, Flesch reading ease, how often they say "you," how often they ask questions, how often they give orders. The Tao Te Ching is 10,432 words at a reading ease of 69.6. Beyond Good and Evil runs 42 words a sentence at a reading ease of 29.8 — the hardest text on the shelf — and Nietzsche still pulls 187,000 views a month, fifth in the whole panel. A two-feature model trained on readability and second-person address predicts the other texts' attention *worse than guessing the average* (leave-one-out R² of −4.0 on n=8; the sample is small and I am treating it as suggestive, not proof). Marcus Aurelius talks to himself in the second person more than anyone — 22.7 "you"s per thousand words, a diary that coaches its author — and readers overwhelmingly look up the man (234k a month) rather than the Meditations (42k). The packaging is the product. The prose is not.

## The honest limits

Wikipedia pageviews are lookup intent on the English Wikipedia, nothing more. They miss book sales, podcasts, YouTube, TikTok, and every language that is not English — which matters a lot for a panel where half the traditions started in Chinese, Sanskrit, and Pali. "Mindfulness fell" means *encyclopedia lookups* fell; the Calm-app explanation is my inference from that shape, not a number in this data. The stylometry is eight texts in old translations — Legge's Tao is Victorian English wearing Chinese clothes, Arnold's Gita is a poem — so treat the stylometry result as a well-measured suggestion, not a verdict. Two joins attach a text to a person article rather than a work article (the Analects to Confucius, Emerson's Essays to Emerson), because that is where the attention actually lives, which is arguably finding 3 wearing a lab coat. And single-month spikes happen for earthly reasons: Marcus Aurelius hit 526,000 views in November 2024, the month a certain Roman sequel was in theaters. I did not chase every one of those. I only quarantined the one that changed the conclusions.

## What it's worth

Philosophy is a real attention market — 453 million lookups on 36 articles in ten years, on one website, in one language — and it behaves like every other attention market I have operated in. Demand concentrates on people and products, not categories. It spikes on a calendar (January resolve, September homework) and on shocks (a pandemic sent people to Epictetus, not to a breathing exercise). It migrates off the page the moment a subscription product can absorb it, which is what the mindfulness curve actually is. And it currently has exactly one compounding incumbent: a shelf of dead Romans whose pitch — control what you can, endure what you can't — turned out to be the only one that a crisis made *more* valuable. The Tao has been saying a quieter version of that for 2,500 years and the Tao is not compounding. Same shelf. Different packaging. The market has rendered its verdict, and like most market verdicts, it is not about who was right.
