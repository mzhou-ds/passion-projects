# Apples2Apples

**An MRI that Medicare values at $224 got billed to me at $7,605 — and the only people allowed to negotiate were my insurer and the hospital. Apples2Apples is the agent that gives the patient a seat at that table before care happens, by running a reverse auction for written Good Faith Estimates.**

*Musing with Mike · 2026-10-08 · Topic: health-care marketplaces (a swap — this one wouldn't wait for the rotation)*

In April I had an MRI of my chin and jaw at UCSF Radiology China Basin — face and neck, no contrast, the kind of scan radiology schedulers book between coffee and lunch. When the bill settled, the anatomy looked like this: UCSF billed **$7,605**. Aetna, my insurer, negotiated it down by $6,850.30. My share was **$754.70** — $400 of deductible plus $354.70 of coinsurance. And Medicare's price for that exact exam, CPT 70540? About **$224**. The federal government buys this scan for $224. My leftover share *alone*, after my insurance company did its job, was more than three times the entire Medicare price. Aetna got a 90% discount off the sticker and I got a bill that was still 3.4 times what the scan costs the biggest buyer in the country. Everyone at that table negotiated. I just wasn't at it.

I've spent my career around marketplaces — Lyft, DoorDash, Patreon, now Meta — and I know what a market looks like when one side can't see prices until after delivery. It looks like American health care. The No Surprises Act technically hands uninsured and self-pay patients a lever: since 2022, any provider has to give you a written Good Faith Estimate, in writing, within three business days of asking, and if the final bill lands $400 or more over that estimate, you can haul it into a federal dispute process. That's a real right. Almost nobody uses it, because exercising it means calling eight imaging centers, spelling a CPT code eight times, and begging eight billing departments to put a number in an email. The law built a marketplace and forgot to build anyone a way into it. So I built the way in — an agent that does the calling, drafting, comparing, and paper-keeping, so by the time you book the scan, clinics have already bid against each other for your business. Apples to apples, same code, same benefits, lowest real price to the patient wins. What follows is the working prototype, run against my actual bill.

## Key findings

**1. The sticker price is fiction, and the fiction is load-bearing.** $7,605 for an exam Medicare prices at $224 is not a price — it's an opening bid in a negotiation I wasn't invited to. Aetna's $6,850.30 "adjustment" isn't a discount they earned me; it's the documented difference between the number on the chargemaster and the number anyone actually pays. The whole apparatus — list price, negotiated rate, allowed amount, my 20% of a number I never saw — exists so that every professional in the chain can negotiate with full information while the person financing the procedure does arithmetic on a bill that arrives weeks later. When SF hospitals publish their cash prices for a brain MRI, the same code spans $2,810 to $6,946 across town. Price dispersion that wide isn't a market. It's a market waiting for someone to run an auction.

**2. UCSF's own filing beats UCSF's own bill.** This morning's fair-price engine (in the repo as benchmarks.md, every figure tied to a public source) pulls what hospitals publish about themselves under the federal price-transparency rules. UCSF's published cash price for a brain MRI without contrast is $432. For a knee MRI, $330. For the same brain code, Stanford Health Care Tri-Valley publishes $2,769. These are the hospitals' own numbers, in their own federal filings — and they sit far below the billed charges that seed the whole negotiation. My tool sets a "fair band" at one to two times the Medicare rate, which is where commercial contracts for imaging actually land. A $754.70 patient share on a $224 exam isn't inside that band. It isn't near that band. It's the band viewed from a great height.

**3. The law protects the catastrophe and ignores the haircut.** The federal dispute right only opens when a final bill is $400 or more over a written estimate. Run my numbers through that filter: even a patient who'd done everything right — requested the estimate, booked the independent center — and still got billed my $754.70 against a $448 top-of-band estimate is looking at a gap the law mostly shrugs at, because the dispute compares against the *estimate you got*, and the estimate itself was priced in the same inflated market. The $400 threshold sounds protective until you realize it legalizes every overcharge beneath it. For the average person getting an ordinary scan, the protection is a velvet rope around a room they're never in. Which, honestly, is the business case: people will pay a small, predictable amount to *not* need rescuing — to insure themselves against the haircut, not just the catastrophe — if someone will do the tedious work of getting prices in writing before the appointment.

**4. The auction, run on my own case, isn't close.** Feed the agent an Aetna plan snapshot ($400 deductible remaining, 20% coinsurance), CPT 70540, a 15-mile radius around 2 Townsend, and three illustrative bid slots priced off published figures: a $499 freestanding-center cash price (RadNet's published rate for the sibling brain code is $550; freestanding face/neck cash listings run $499), a $620 independent-center bid, an $863 hospital direct-pay listing. The board ranks by what *I'd* pay, not by sticker: Clinic C's $620 bid becomes a $444 patient share after deductible and coinsurance; the hospital's $863 becomes $492.60; the cash option stays $499. Best bid: **$444, or $310.70 less than the $754.70 I actually paid** for the identical exam — and that's before the winner has to defend its number in writing against two rivals. These bids are illustrative, labeled as such in every row of the output. But the spread isn't an artifact of my demo numbers. It's the real, published dispersion of prices for the same magnet, the same code, the same city.

## What I built

| # | Pipeline stage | What it does | Code |
|---|----------------|--------------|------|
| 1 | **Intake** | Plan snapshot from JSON: insurer, deductible remaining, coinsurance %, out-of-pocket max remaining, location, radius, and what you actually paid last time. No accounts, no credentials, no scraping. | `apples2apples.py` (`run`, `patient_share`) |
| 2 | **CPT decomposition** | Plain language ("chin mri") → billable codes with the traps flagged: combined codes that must not be unbundled (70551+70552 = 70553), -26 professional-component splits, TMJ vs. face/neck study confusion. | `DECOMPOSITION` table, `--procedure` mode |
| 3 | **Benchmarking** | Every CPT priced against sourced Medicare allowed amounts, the hospital's own published cash price, and a fair band of 1.0–2.0× Medicare. Where no source exists, the tool says "not captured" instead of inventing a number. | `BENCHMARKS` table + `benchmarks.md` |
| 4 | **Clinic scan** | Candidate sites within the radius, each carrying its basis label. Demo sites are illustrative slots with published-price anchors; production plugs in transparency files and insurer network data. | intake `clinics` list |
| 5 | **Outreach drafts** | Per site: one email draft + one 60-second call script requesting a written Good Faith Estimate with the full CPT stack and expected patient share. **Drafts only — the program has no send function, on purpose.** | `email_draft`, `call_script` |
| 6 | **Auction + procedure calculator** | Bids ranked by expected patient share under *your* plan — deductible first, then coinsurance, capped at your out-of-pocket remaining — with insurer share, benchmark deltas, and savings vs. your last bill. | bid board in `run` |
| 7 | **Paper-trail packet** | Intake, every draft, every bid, timestamps, recommendation, decision log — one file, so if a bill ever lands $400+ over a written estimate, the dispute folder already exists. | `--packet` output |

Run it: `python3 apples2apples.py --intake demo/intake-michael-70540.json --bids demo/bids-illustrative.json --packet paper-trail-70540.md` (full output in `demo-run-70540.md`). Or sanity-check a single procedure: `python3 apples2apples.py --procedure "knee mri"`.

## The honest limits

The demo bids are illustrative, and I haven't hidden that anywhere — each row of the bid board says so, because a tool about price honesty doesn't get to fake its own prices. The $499/$620/$863 slots are anchored to published figures (freestanding cash listings, hospital direct-pay schedules), but no clinic has quoted Michael Zhou anything; the word "bid" describes the mechanism, not a completed transaction. The clinic names are slots, not businesses, and nobody's in-network status on my specific Aetna plan has been verified — that's a production integration, not a demo omission. The benchmark table uses national Medicare references; California runs 10–15% hotter, which makes my fair band conservative — it argues *against* me, which is how I prefer my math.

Two legal honesties. The federal Good Faith Estimate right covers the uninsured and self-pay; as an insured patient, my lever is weaker — an insurer's written estimate plus genuine price competition — and the tool's drafts ask for exactly that without pretending the insured case has the same statutory teeth. And the picture that started this piece, my $754.70 share, is only fully adjudicable with the itemized statement and the EOB in hand; billed charges, allowed amounts, and patient balances are three different animals, and disputes are strongest against the third with paperwork behind them. Nothing in this repo sends an email, places a call, or touches my UCSF account. The outreach is draft-only because a paper trail you didn't approve isn't a paper trail — it's a liability.

## What it's worth

Three ways to charge for it, and they stack. A consumer can pay a percentage of verified savings — post-bill bill-negotiators already charge 20–25%, and intervening *before* the bill, when the leverage is real, is worth at least the 10–20% modeled here. Clinics can pay per booked, pre-priced patient — MDsave already runs provider-side transaction fees, because a patient who arrives with an agreed written price is the cheapest patient a clinic ever acquires. And the scale path is employers: Healthcare Bluebook's client data put up to 12% of an employer's total medical spend within reach of exactly this kind of shopping, and self-funded employers buy against that number every renewal season. What production needs is unglamorous and known: clinic outreach integrations (email APIs and, yes, actual phone agents for the billing departments that never answer email), EOB and plan-document parsing so the intake fills itself, network-status verification against plan data, and a clinic-side claim flow that turns "illustrative slot" into a name with a scheduler. None of that is research. All of it is plumbing. The research — does price competition even exist to be harvested? — my own bill already answered. A $224 scan got sold three times on its way to me. Apples2Apples is just the first time the patient gets to run the auction instead of funding it.
