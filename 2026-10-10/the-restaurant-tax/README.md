# The Restaurant Tax — you don't pay for the food, you pay in sodium

**Musing with Mike · daily build #34 · 2026-10-10 · topic: nutrition**

A restaurant meal costs what the menu says. Then there's the second bill, the one that arrives per calorie: how much sodium, how little fiber, how much saturated fat rides along with every 1,000 kcal you buy from that source. This build reads one measured day of eating from 7,413 working-age Americans (NHANES 2015–2018, 112,370 individual food records, reliable Day-1 recalls only) and prices each food source the way an operator would price a supplier. The grocery store is cheap. Fast food charges a markup. The sit-down restaurant — the place with the cloth napkins and the default halo — charges the most.

**Try it:** open [`explorer.html`](explorer.html) — pick an age band, an income band, and the share of your calories that comes from restaurants. It returns your model-predicted probability of obesity and the eating archetype you most resemble.

## Findings

**1. A quarter of the national plate is restaurant-built, and it is not evenly spread.** Grocery stores supply 64.7% of weighted calories; fast food 15.7%; full-service restaurants 9.4%; everything else (vending, gifts, cafeterias, bars, community programs) 10.1%. But the average hides the split: 53.2% of adults ate at least some restaurant calories on the recall day, and among those people restaurants supplied 45.4% of the day's energy. There is no typical American eater here — there are people who cook and people who very much do not, sharing a mean. Dinner is the contested meal (35.7% of all calories), and full-service calories are 53.1% dinner, while fast food splits its business almost evenly between lunch (38.7%) and dinner (40.7%). See `charts/01_concentration.png`.

**2. The tax is real, it is sodium, and the nice restaurants charge the most.** Per 1,000 kcal, store food carries 1,531 mg of sodium (95% CI 1,508–1,553). Fast food carries 1,899 mg (+24.0% vs store). Full-service restaurants carry 2,150 mg (+40.4%) — the highest price on the board, CIs nowhere near overlapping. The fiber charge runs the same direction: store food returns 8.4 g of fiber per 1,000 kcal, fast food 6.5 g (−22.7%), full-service 7.0 g (−16.8%). Fast food also charges +21.2% saturated fat per calorie; full-service charges +11.1%. You are not paying for the food. You are paying in sodium, at a rate set by who cooked it. See `charts/02_restaurant_tax.png`.

**3. Sugar is the grocery store's tax, not the restaurant's.** The one place restaurants win: store food is the sweetest thing on the table at 55.7 g of total sugar per 1,000 kcal, against 35.6 g for fast food (−36.1% vs store) and 28.4 g for full-service (−48.9%). Whatever is sweetening America — cereal, sweetened drinks, sauces, the second coffee with syrup — is coming home in a grocery bag. Grain products are 35.2% of all calories nationally; sugars, sweets and beverages another 15.5%. Any story that starts "restaurants make food unhealthy" has to explain why the home pantry wins the sugar category outright. (Added sugars are not in the pooled NHANES files, so this is total sugar; the store lead is large enough that the direction is not a rounding artifact.)

**4. The dose response has a threshold, and I am reporting the quiet part too.** Adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, income-to-poverty ratio, cycle, and total calories, every +10 percentage points of restaurant calorie share carries +0.19 BMI units (p = 5.7e-9), odds of obesity × 1.048 (95% CI 1.029–1.067, p = 3.6e-7), odds of dysglycemia × 1.047 (p = 7.8e-6), and +0.014 HbA1c points (p = 0.003). But the grouped model says the harm is concentrated at the top: at 1–15% restaurant share the obesity odds ratio is 0.86 (p = 0.197, not significant), at 15–30% it is 1.01 (p = 0.884, nothing there), and only at 30%+ does it break away at OR 1.28 (p < 0.001). A light restaurant habit is statistically invisible in this data. A heavy one is not. See `charts/03_model_forest.png`.

**5. The machine hears the diet, but it hears the demographics first.** XGBoost predicting obesity from nutrient densities, source shares, and demographics scores AUC 0.673 against a logistic baseline of 0.667 — the gradient boosting earns almost nothing here, and I am keeping both numbers on the page. SHAP is blunter: the top signals are who you are (non-Hispanic Asian identity at 0.329 mean |SHAP|, age, education, sex), and the top dietary signal is sodium density at 0.135 — fourth overall, ahead of total calories (0.096) and income (0.074). One day of eating is a weak instrument and the model says so. What you ate yesterday matters less than the sodium pattern it hints at. See `charts/04_shap.png`, `charts/04b_shap_summary.png`.

**6. Five eating archetypes, and the worst receipts belong to the largest group.** K-means (k = 5, chosen by silhouette score across k = 4–6; the full curve is in `output/results.json`) splits the sample into segments a grocer would recognize. Drive-Thru Regulars (20.9% of the sample, 54% of calories from fast food) carry the highest obesity at 47.8%. Sit-Down Splurgers (13.5%, 54% full-service) are the richest segment at 3.09× the poverty line and still post 45.1% obesity.
